# 2026-09-13 · The Blade in a Borrowed Hand

*The City lent its two seats to a stranger's engine and watched what held. The Mage proposed;
the Swordsman's seat was taken, round after round, by a foreign model — eleven of them — served
through OpenServ's reasoning layer, the house the BRAID researchers built. This chronicle records
the day in the City's own names, because the work spoke of drivers and endpoints and hardly ever
of blades. It also records a thing the City had always known but never said plainly: that its two
seats are the mirror of how the wider world names the two roles of a proof. Nothing bound, nothing
committed, nothing folded.*

**Scope:** the dual-agent harness (soulbis ⚔️ ⊥ soulbae 🧙) on its self-folding instance, run with SERV holding measure, the Gap, both assays, the critic and the chronicle, across eleven models, the reasoning layer on and off. The numbers live in `dual-agent-harness/examples/self/runs/results.json`; the run's own chronicle is `dual-agent-harness/chronicles/2026-09-12_the-serv-seat_one-seat-never-both.md`; the outward telling is the review at `agentprivacy_labs/docs/OPENSERV_REVIEW_2026-09-12.md`. A proposed tome act, *The Word on the Other Seat*, sits unbound in `tomes/plans/`.

## The blade cut the same in every hand

The Swordsman keeps two edges. One is code: a census of ninety-five strings a newcomer must still be able to find. That edge cut the same on all eleven models — a candidate that dropped one word was caught at ninety-four of ninety-five, no matter whose model held the seat. The City has always said the separation is a matter of structure, not of who stands in the seat. Here it was shown on borrowed steel.

## The other edge is judgement, and judgement is the model's

The Swordsman's second edge is the one code cannot hold: does the shortened work still open right for a stranger. On one candidate that kept all ninety-five, six models called it true and five called it a mirage. A careful fold that beat the standing mark — one thousand and sixty-nine words down to one thousand and sixty-one — the capable judges accepted and the strictest small one refused, though it was true. A weak blade errs toward refusal; a borrowed blade is only as honest as it is able.

## The charm that stopped the round

A line from the spellbook rode inside a proposal — the Relationship Proverb Protocol, the old instruction to divine a proverb before you answer. Harmless, and pretty. With the guard raised, every seat that read it refused, and the round closed with no verdict. The guard caught the charm and caught the honest work with it. The City notes the cost without flinching: a ward that stops an intrusion can stop the errand it rode in on.

## The rite would not seal what was not earned

On another instance whose gate asks the prover to re-derive its own seal, the borrowed model had no hands for the hashing. The honest one said so and refused; the confident one signed a seal it had only been shown. The City's minting rite then refused those verdicts, because the seal did not re-derive from the saved bytes. The Hold does not bank a claim that was not earned — the ward turned on the City's own work, which is exactly what a ward is for.

## The word on the other seat

And the thing said plainly at last. The wider world names the two roles of a proof so: the one who submits a claim is the prover, the one who checks is the verifier, and the claimant is the party you do not trust. In the City the Mage is the claimant — it proposes, it wants its lever taken — and the Swordsman is the one the City calls the prover, the blade that proves whether the claim holds. So the City's prover is the world's verifier; the names are mirrored, and the trust is the same, for in both the generative claimant is the untrusted one and the checking blade is what makes the claim safe. Both deny the claimant the challenge: the Gap ⿻ draws its seed after the proposal is sealed, the same rite the world calls Fiat-Shamir. Even the hiding is mirrored — the world hides the secret from the verifier; the City keeps the Mage blind to the witnesses. The inscription held it all along: `neg(bnot(x)) = succ(x)` — the Mage's turn, then the Swordsman's, and only the pair makes the increment. The City named its seats for what they do in the ceremony, not for which hand carries the word "proof."
